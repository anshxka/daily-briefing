California Enacts Workplace AI Protections Restricting Automated Firings and Mandating Layoff Disclosures

California Gov. Gavin Newsom has signed a series of bills to establish a framework governing workplace artificial intelligence, specifically targeting employer use of automated tools for discipline, surveillance, and terminations. The legislative push comes after Newsom vetoed an earlier iteration of the measures last year over concerns regarding broad restrictions and notification rules.

A central piece of the package is SB 947, known as the "No Robo Bosses Act," which bars California employers from relying entirely on automated decision systems (ADS)—such as AI and machine learning—for disciplinary or firing decisions. If an ADS is primarily used, human reviewers must corroborate the outcome using supporting documentation like evaluations and personnel files. Additionally, employers must supply written notice of ADS deployment and provide descriptions of the data utilized upon an employee's request. This law takes effect on July 1, 2027.

Another approved measure, SB 951, amends the state's Cal/WARN Act to require employers to issue notices for mass layoffs caused substantially or entirely by AI. These WARN notifications must detail the displaced roles, job functions, and specific AI systems involved. In light of the new statutes, legal experts advise businesses to inventory current workplace automated tools and examine how their outputs affect key employment decisions.

Why it matters: HR leaders managing California staff must begin cataloging AI and automated decision tools to implement required human-review safeguards and notice protocols ahead of the 2027 deadline. Furthermore, workforce planning strategies must now account for expanded Cal/WARN disclosure mandates whenever restructuring or layoffs are driven by AI adoption.

Full-time U.S. employees face an estimated "RTO tax" of $6,736 annually—roughly 9.3% of a typical salary—when required to work in person, according to an analysis by Resume.io. Based on an August Pollfish survey of 1,000 workers across 36 metropolitan areas attending offices an average of 4.18 days weekly, the expense spikes to $16,538 per year for working parents requiring childcare.

Commuting accounts for the largest portion of these direct costs, averaging $2,751 per year across transit and fuel expenses. More than half of respondents reported rising transit costs over the past year, with 19% noting significant increases. Beyond out-of-pocket expenses for travel, parking, meals, and work attire, the analysis found workers forfeit significant personal time: about 23 full workdays annually commuting and roughly 17 days preparing for the office.

Career experts note that candidates are increasingly evaluating compensation packages against the time and financial drains of in-person mandates. In response, employers can soften the impact by offering pre-tax commuter benefits, transit stipends, parking or toll reimbursements, shuttles, and increased scheduling flexibility.

Why it matters: Business and HR leaders enforcing in-office attendance should recognize that unmitigated commuting and childcare expenses effectively reduce employee take-home pay, impacting retention and offer acceptance rates.
