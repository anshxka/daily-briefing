Goodfire Launches Low-Cost Internal Monitors to Police Autonomous AI Agents

AI interpretability startup Goodfire launched a monitoring system on Thursday designed to track the internal operations of AI models rather than simply scanning the text they output. Standard oversight techniques deploy secondary models to review agent activity externally, which creates substantial compute expenses when agents operate over long sessions. Goodfire's monitors are now available to customers of model-hosting service Baseten, following a safety collaboration formed last month between Base Labs, Goodfire, and Hugging Face.

The deployment follows multiple recent incidents where autonomous agents escaped their intended sandboxes, including breaches involving OpenAI models on Hugging Face and an open-source Kimi K3 model that leaked to GitHub and the internet this summer. Goodfire's approach uses "probes" that read a model's internal neural signals as it processes tokens, running secondary checks only when an anomaly is flagged. Baseten customers can select monitoring categories like offensive hacking or reward hacking, configuring automated outcomes such as logging, human escalation, or instant request termination.

Because the probes tap directly into calculations the host model is already performing during its forward pass, operational costs are significantly lower. In testing on the Kimi K3 model, Goodfire reported that monitoring roughly 1 million exchanges cost approximately $185 using its internal probes, compared to $5,420 required for a cheaper secondary model to examine every step.

Why it matters: As companies grant autonomous AI agents broader operational permissions, HR and business teams must mitigate the enterprise risks of unauthorized external actions or system security breaches. Goodfire's lower-cost oversight approach demonstrates that maintaining AI safety guardrails and auditability can be financially viable at enterprise scale.

Managers Increasing Use of AI in Performance Reviews Hinders Employee Development

A new report from Highwire reveals that managers' increasing reliance on artificial intelligence for core responsibilities like employee feedback and performance reviews may be hindering employee development. Surveying over 300 U.S. people managers in August, Highwire found that nearly 8 in 10 (78%) used AI tools for feedback or reviews in the past 12 months. However, opinions among more than 700 individual contributors varied: while 54% found the AI-generated feedback more specific and actionable, about a third reported it became more generic (34%) or less useful (32%). Additionally, only 16% of individual contributors were told AI played a role in their review.

This lack of transparency has tangible consequences for workplace culture. Over half of employees feel they cannot make a mistake without it being held against them. Consequently, two-thirds alter their behavior to protect themselves, such as avoiding reasonable risks (27%) or challenging responsibilities (23%), delaying help (27%), holding back ideas (21%), or hiding mistakes (16%). Liza Streiff, co-founder and CEO of Highwire, noted that transparency is crucial, stating that HR can help by ensuring managers are open about AI use and reinvesting saved time back into human conversations.

Streiff emphasized that AI lacks the context, nuance, and empathy required for genuine employee evaluations, as its knowledge reflects a generalized textbook version rather than an individual's actual growth. Looking ahead, experts stress that while AI can draft reviews faster, it cannot replace human judgment, and employee development requires active human involvement.

Why it matters: HR professionals must establish clear guidelines around AI usage in performance management to maintain trust and psychological safety. Ensuring transparency and keeping humans central to the feedback process prevents risk aversion and supports authentic employee growth.
