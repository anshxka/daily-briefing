Anthropic Proposes AI Safety Bill Restricting High-Risk Model Releases

Artificial intelligence lab Anthropic has drafted a federal AI safety bill and is circulating it to members of Congress, proposing statutory restrictions on distributing models that pose severe threats. Under the draft legislation, the release of an AI model could be blocked if it presents an imminent and foreseeable risk of causing more than 50 deaths or exceeding $1 billion in property damage. Such catastrophic hazards must specifically stem from biological weapons, cyberattacks, or a model evading developer controls.

The proposed metrics align closely with California’s recently enacted Senate Bill 53, which established similar thresholds for catastrophic risk. To evaluate model safety, Anthropic's proposal suggests using independent evaluators registered with the U.S. Commerce Department. AI labs would be permitted to pay these evaluators directly, provided the compensation is not contingent upon the evaluation results.

Under the framework, evaluators identifying an imminent catastrophic risk would have 72 hours to notify the Commerce Department. The department would then need to petition a Washington, D.C., judge to halt the model's release, expressly barring any federal agency from independently halting distribution. Anthropic confirmed the draft bill to Politico, stating the proposal reflects the Advanced AI Framework it has championed with lawmakers.

Why it matters: As major AI labs actively lobby for statutory risk thresholds, business leaders should anticipate formal federal compliance standards for high-impact AI deployments. Department of Labor Rescinds Decades-Old Dual-Job Tip Rule Opinion Letters

The U.S. Department of Labor has withdrawn three opinion letters dating from 1979, 1980, and 1985 regarding how the Fair Labor Standards Act applies to tipped employees working dual-job roles. Alongside the rescissions, the agency issued a field assistance bulletin clarifying its updated enforcement stance regarding employers taking a tip credit against the federal minimum wage for workers performing both tipped and nontipped tasks.

Under federal standards, dual-job employees may only receive the federal tipped minimum wage of $2.13 per hour for the time they spend actively engaged in tipped work, requiring full minimum wage for all other hours. However, federal regulations permit employers to pay the tipped rate when workers execute related tasks that do not directly generate tips, such as a waitress setting tables or brewing coffee, provided those tasks are part of the tipped occupation.

The regulatory approach to dual-job tip credits has seen repeated administrative swings over several decades. After the Department of Labor long enforced the '80/20' rule—capping nontipped related tasks at 20 percent of working time to qualify for the tip credit—the first Trump administration attempted to abandon the restriction, leading to a 2020 final rule. Although the Biden administration later reinstated the 80/20 standard, the 5th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals struck down the regulation in 2024 for conflicting with the text of the FLSA.

Why it matters: Employers operating with tipped staff must closely monitor administrative guidance to ensure wage practices align with current federal enforcement and recent judicial rulings. Proper tracking of hours and job duties remains critical to avoid minimum-wage liability under the Fair Labor Standards Act.
