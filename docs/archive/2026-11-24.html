Lufthansa Plans 1,000 Job Cuts Amid Slow Turnaround and Energy Pressures

German airline Lufthansa is planning to cut 1,000 full-time positions at its Frankfurt and Munich airports, which amounts to roughly 2.5 percent of its approximately 40,000 employees. The carrier stated that its 2024 cost-cutting programme to stem losses is "no longer sufficient" as turnaround efforts move slower than hoped. During the first nine months of the year, the airline lost an average of 20 million euros ($22 million) a month.

According to an internal letter, the situation has been intensified by the war in the Middle East, high fuel costs, nine days of strike action, and an already very high cost base. Surging fuel costs since the start of the US war on Iran are expected to bring the group's annual fuel bill in two billion euros higher this year than in 2025. Despite these challenges at the flagship carrier, the wider group reported a 30-percent jump in profits during the July-September period, beating forecasts due to demand for premium tickets on long-haul routes.

It remains unclear whether the newly disclosed cuts are included in a previously announced plan to cut 4,000 jobs across the whole group by 2030, or if they are in addition to them. A Lufthansa spokesman declined to provide further details regarding the layoffs.

Why it matters: This move highlights how macroeconomic shocks and geopolitical conflicts can quickly undermine long-term restructuring plans, forcing leadership to reconsider headcount and operational footprints. India’s Rupee Becomes Accidental Carry Star Driven by Central Bank Measures

Seventeen years later, the fever is back as the Indian rupee delivers the world’s highest returns on US dollar-funded carry trades this quarter. Foreign investors are rotating out of artificial intelligence stocks and into underperforming markets like India. However, this run is unusual because the rupee has rarely been a favored carry currency despite India's high interest rates and fast-growing major economy status.

The recent strength is largely driven by the Reserve Bank of India's (RBI) campaign to curb speculation against the currency, which includes encouraging dollar inflows through overseas borrowing and making it harder to short the rupee. Bank of America strategists note this has created negative carry for bears, making short bets prohibitively expensive. Since September, about four-fifths of the gains from rupee carry have come from currency appreciation rather than interest income, supported by falling oil prices and a broader rally in Indian stocks and bonds.

Analysts caution that carry traders are making a tourist stop in India, drawn by temporary RBI inducements that are due to expire later this year. Once those measures disappear, investors will have to confront fundamentals, as the yield pickup alone may not compensate for historical depreciation, volatility, and geopolitical risks.
