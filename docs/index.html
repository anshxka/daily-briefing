Appeals Court Rules Mandatory Waiting Time Is Compensable Under FLSA

A divided federal appeals court has revived a collective action wage and hour lawsuit against Lidl, ruling that employees must be compensated for time spent waiting if they are required to report to work and cannot perform duties due to circumstances beyond their control.

In Santos v. Lidl, grocery workers alleged they were mandated to show up 15 minutes prior to their morning shifts. However, managers routinely arrived late to unlock the store doors, leaving workers stranded in the parking lot and unable to clock in. A lower court initially dismissed the case, classifying the waiting period as a noncompensable preliminary activity under the Portal-to-Portal Act's amendments to the Fair Labor Standards Act. Lidl maintained that workers did not need to be paid because they were free to read or leave the premises.

The 2nd U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals vacated and remanded the decision, relying on federal regulations that differentiate between an employee who is "engaged to wait" versus one who "waited to be engaged." The court emphasized that when an employer requires a worker to report at a designated time and place, and the employee is ready and willing to work, waiting becomes an integral part of their principal activities.

Why it matters: Employers must review scheduling policies and shift-start routines to ensure mandatory arrival times are fully compensated even when facilities are inaccessible. Failure to align timekeeping systems with required reporting expectations creates significant exposure to collective wage-and-hour liability.
