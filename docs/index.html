Apollo Economist Reports Zero Financial Payoff from AI for Non-Tech Corporate America

Artificial intelligence has failed to improve the bottom line of major corporations outside the tech sector, according to a recent analysis by Torsten Slok, chief economist at Apollo Global Management. Examining what he designates as the 'S&P 493'—the S&P 500 excluding Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, and Tesla—Slok found that profit margins and revenue per worker have remained flat, with no clear evidence of productivity gains.

Despite heavy spending and industry-wide enthusiasm, Slok stated that AI has provided zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of the primary tech giants. He expressed skepticism that non-tech businesses are simply lagging behind, warning that converting increasingly cheap machine intelligence into higher revenue growth and stronger margins will be exceptionally difficult.

Slok highlighted critical structural limitations of the technology, noting that while AI functions as a powerful calculator, real-world business relies heavily on human judgment. AI does not comprehend trust, accountability, workplace culture, ethics, or unwritten rules, all of which mitigate reputational risks and ultimately drive corporate profitability. Other recent studies similarly indicate that broad AI productivity gains have yet to materialize.

Why it matters: This indicates that HR and business leaders should scrutinize AI productivity claims and ensure investments are backed by clear, measurable business metrics rather than hype. It also highlights that uniquely human skills—such as ethical judgment, accountability, and cultural stewardship—remain essential drivers of business performance that technology cannot replicate.

SpaceX Closes $40 Billion Off-Balance-Sheet Debt Deal to Fund AI Chips

SpaceX has secured a $40 billion off-balance-sheet debt deal to acquire Nvidia graphics processing units (GPUs) for its artificial intelligence data centers, according to company owner Elon Musk and sources reported by Bloomberg. Musk stated that the company was able to complete the transaction because SpaceX holds an investment-grade standing supported by ongoing revenue from its Starlink and rocket launch divisions.

Bankers led by Goldman Sachs Group Inc. negotiated the financing structure with investors led by Apollo Global Management Inc., with Apollo-backed Atlas SP slated to manage the entity and Pacific Investment Management Co. reviewing participation. The debt was slated for issuance as Rule 144a private placements. However, pricing discussions faced complications amid growing market turmoil surrounding AI infrastructure and high borrowing costs across the industry.

Musk contrasted SpaceX with specialized computing providers known as 'neoclouds,' which face intense scrutiny and restricted debt markets due to a lack of independent revenue. Musk predicted severe industry consolidation and failures among more than 200 neoclouds, confirming SpaceX has no intention of acquiring them. The off-balance-sheet setup is also designed to avoid past issues faced by xAI, where an attempted $20 billion leaseback financing resulted in $13 billion landing on SpaceX's balance sheet.

Why it matters: The deal shows that massive capital allocation for AI infrastructure is increasingly constrained to organizations with diversified, reliable cash flows rather than standalone tech ventures. Business leaders must monitor this financing squeeze, as rising infrastructure costs and market consolidation among compute providers could impact enterprise AI development budgets and software supply chains.
