Pennsylvania Enacts Law Requiring Disclosures on AI-Generated Advertisements

Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro has signed the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act, making the state one of the earliest to mandate formal disclosures on commercial content created with synthetic media. The legislation applies broadly across formats, requiring any print, audio, text, or video advertisement that has been significantly modified or generated by AI to feature an explicit disclosure.

The law enforces compliance across all marketing tiers, covering everything from multi-billion-dollar corporate television campaigns to flyers distributed by local small businesses. Furthermore, the requirement applies regardless of where an advertisement was originally produced, meaning marketing materials designed in other jurisdictions must incorporate labels if they are slated to air or circulate within Pennsylvania.

Despite its passage, the statute contains several subjective definitions and potential loopholes. The law does not clearly define what constitutes "significantly modified" material, and it provides exemptions for parody, satire, criticism, and content deemed "evidently fictional." Additionally, the language suggests enforcement may primarily target content that utilizes synthetic elements to create false consumer perceptions about products or services.

As the act goes into effect, legal and marketing teams will face ambiguities regarding how regulatory bodies interpret false perception and fictional content. The office of State Senator Nick Pisciottano, who introduced the measure, had not commented on the specific enforcement timeline or clarifying criteria.

Why it matters: Marketing, legal, and HR compliance departments will need to establish strict internal review protocols to audit any promotional materials featuring AI-generated media. Because the law targets where ads are shown rather than where they are made, national campaigns will have to adjust disclosures across broad multi-state distribution channels.

Block Introduces Dessa, an Emotion-Driven Voice AI Running on Local Hardware

Block, the financial technology firm led by Jack Dorsey, has unveiled a new voice AI model named Dessa from its internal research lab. Trained entirely on human speech using a single Nvidia GPU rack, the system operates as a full-duplex model capable of listening and speaking simultaneously, incorporating conversational nuances such as laughter, sighs, crying, and natural pauses.

Unlike traditional voice systems that pipe text through an underlying large language model or rely on external emotion tags, Block designed Dessa to derive its knowledge and emotional cadence directly from conversational audio data. Company researchers argue that converting voice to text strips out critical emotional context, making typical enterprise voice agents frustrating and potentially hazardous for consumers.

Demonstrations released by the research team showcase Dessa adapting dynamically to varied conversational contexts, including altering its tone to express fatigue or displaying emotional vulnerability during interactions. Block stated that the model is efficient enough to run locally on consumer-grade computing hardware, such as an Apple MacBook M4 Max, avoiding the need for continuous cloud server processing.

While Dessa is not yet available to the public, Block has opened access to external AI safety researchers to test risks, specifically around voice deception. The technology holds direct operational relevance for Block's commercial ecosystem, including Square's automated food ordering operations, by offering lower-cost, highly responsive on-device voice interactions.

Why it matters: For HR and operations leaders, low-cost local voice models like Dessa could radically alter the economics and implementation of customer service roles and internal interactive training. However, the eerily human emotional delivery also introduces corporate risks regarding consumer trust, deepfake potential, and ethical communication standards.
